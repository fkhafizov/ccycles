Graph ↔ Rectangle Holography for cc2

Distance in the cc2 graph G₂(n,k) without searching it. Like measuring how far apart two towns are by laying their maps on top of each other instead of driving the roads: each vertex becomes a staircase in a k × (n−k) box, and d(X,Y) is the number of unit boxes between the two staircases.

      Vertices and edges: G₂(n,k) has the binary words of length n with k zeros as vertices; two words are joined when an adjacent swap 01 ↔ 10 turns one into the other.

      Holography map: X ↦ profile a(X) = (a₁, …, a_k), where aᵢ is the number of 1s to the left of the i-th 0, so 0 ≤ a₁ ≤ … ≤ a_k ≤ n−k. Reading the word left to right with 1 = step up and 0 = step right draws a staircase in the k × (n−k) box whose i-th column has height aᵢ.

      Edges = boxes: an adjacent swap changes exactly one aᵢ by ±1, so it adds or removes exactly one unit box under the staircase.

      Complexity = Area: d(X,Y) = Σᵢ |aᵢ(X) − aᵢ(Y)|, the number of boxes between the two staircases. This costs O(k) arithmetic; no search of the C(n,k) vertices is done.

      Shortest walk: lower X column by column (i = 1, …, k) to the meet X ∧ Y with columns min(aᵢ(X), aᵢ(Y)), then raise it to Y (i = k, …, 1). Every intermediate profile stays non-decreasing, and the walk has exactly d steps, so it is a geodesic.

      Sampled view (large n): the left panel shows random vertices placed by layer U (vertical) and a tilt statistic (horizontal). One real edge moves a vertex by only about 1/(k(n−k)) of the picture's height, far less than a pixel, so edges cannot be drawn. The dotted links join each sample to its two nearest samples and usually stand for many steps; hover a link to see its d. Two vertices that look close can still be thousands of steps apart.

      Layouts of the sampled view: Linear plots (U, T) with tilt T = Σ wᵢaᵢ, where the weights wᵢ rise from −1 to 1. Because a non-decreasing profile always has T ≥ 0, with T = 0 only for flat profiles, and the steepest profile in layer U has T ≈ k(n−k)·u(1−u) with u = U/(k(n−k)), the cloud fills the region between a straight left edge (A to Ā) and a parabola: the "triangle". Stretched keeps the vertical coordinate U exactly and rescales T within each layer to run from the flattest (0) to the steepest (1) profile, which fills a square. Even replaces both coordinates by their ranks among the samples; it spreads the dots uniformly but is not to scale. Most vertices of G₂(n,k) are near the uniform-random profile, so a dense cluster stays in every layout: that is a real feature of the graph.

      Layers: U(X) = Σ aᵢ = d(A, X) for the anchor A = 0ᵏ1ⁿ⁻ᵏ (the empty staircase). Hence |U(X) − U(Y)| ≤ d(X,Y), with equality exactly when the staircases do not cross. Its partner Ā = 1ⁿ⁻ᵏ0ᵏ is the full box, at distance k(n−k) from A.
    

  






  

    
How to use

    
    
      
  1. Pick n and k (k ≤ n/2), or a preset. When C(n,k) ≤ 400 the left panel shows the whole graph; otherwise it shows a sample of vertices.
    2. 
      
  2. Click two vertices on the left: the first click sets X, the second sets Y (the button in the panel header shows which comes next). You can also type a vertex: a 0/1 word, a: 0,2,2,5 (the profile), or z: 1,4,6,10 (positions of the zeros).
    3. 
      
  3. The right panel draws both staircases and hatches the boxes between them; their count is d(X,Y).
    4. 
      
  4. Press Play or drag the slider to walk W along a shortest walk. The gold region counts the steps taken: d(X,W) + d(W,Y) = d(X,Y) at every step.
    5. 
      
  5. Hover a vertex on the left to see its distances to X and Y, computed by the same formula.
    6. 
      
  6. Zoom the left panel when X and Y sit too close: scroll over it (zooms around the cursor), use the − and + buttons or keys, and drag to pan. Zoom to X, Y frames X, Y, the meet X ∧ Y and the walk; Fit all (or key 0) returns to the full view. The scale note in the corner updates with the zoom.
    7. 
    

    
For scale: the usual estimate is about 10⁸⁰ atoms in the observable universe, with a commonly quoted range of 10⁷⁸ to 10⁸².
      For scale against the app: at n = 1000, k = 300, the cc2 graph has C(1000, 300) ≈ 5.4 × 10²⁶³ vertices. That’s about 10¹⁸³ times the number of atoms in the observable universe, so BFS was never an option there.

    
The rhomb on the left panel: the meet X ∧ Y

    
The rhomb is the meet X ∧ Y, the vertex where the shortest walk turns around. Its profile is the column-by-column minimum:

    
aᵢ(X ∧ Y) = min(aᵢ(X), aᵢ(Y))

    
On the right panel it is the lower edge of the two staircases: it follows Y wherever Y is below X, and X everywhere else.
      The pink walk from X to Y passes through it in two phases:

    
    
      
  1. X → X ∧ Y: lower the columns of X down to the minimum. This removes the boxes of X that lie above Y and takes Σᵢ max(aᵢ(X) − aᵢ(Y), 0) steps.
    2. 
      
  2. X ∧ Y → Y: raise the columns up to Y. This adds the boxes of Y that lie above X and takes Σᵢ max(aᵢ(Y) − aᵢ(X), 0) steps.
    3. 
    

    
The two parts add up to Σᵢ |aᵢ(X) − aᵢ(Y)| = d(X,Y), so the walk is a geodesic.
      The rhomb sits above both X and Y because the meet has fewer boxes than either: U(X ∧ Y) ≤ min(U(X), U(Y)), and smaller U is drawn higher.
      When you press Play, the walker readout shows “(at X∧Y)” at the moment W reaches it; that is when the gold region on the right stops removing boxes and starts adding them.

    
Why the sampled view looks like a triangle

    
The left panel is a linear projection of each profile a = (a₁, …, a_k) to two numbers. The vertical one is U = Σ aᵢ, and the horizontal one is the tilt T = Σ wᵢaᵢ, with weights wᵢ rising from −1 to 1. Two facts fix the shape:

    
    
      
  • Left edge is straight. Because a is non-decreasing and w is increasing, T ≥ 0, with T = 0 only for flat profiles (all aᵢ equal). This follows from Chebyshev’s sum inequality. Flat profiles in every layer line up on the vertical line from A to Ā.
    • 
      
  • Right edge is a parabola. The steepest profile in layer U puts all its boxes in the last columns, (0, …, 0, r, M, …, M). For it, T / (k(n−k)) ≈ u(1 − u) with u = U / (k(n−k)). So the widest point is the middle layer, and the width shrinks to zero at A and Ā, which are each the only vertex in their layer.
    • 
    

    
So the image is the region 0 ≤ x ≲ y(1 − y). The dense blob is real: almost every vertex of G₂(n,k) is close to the uniform-random profile, aᵢ ≈ i·M/k.

For Stretched, the flattest and steepest profiles in every layer map exactly to 0 and 1 (checked over all 2,002 vertices at n = 14, k = 5).
      The sampler also draws steep profiles with a few big jumps. That fills the parabola side in every layout, so it is a better sample of the vertex set and not only a change in the drawing.
      The walk and the walker W are placed exactly in all three layouts; in the two non-linear layouts the straight segments of the walk bend, so each is drawn as a curve.

    
Is spreading advisable?

    
Use Stretched for reading distances. Its vertical axis is still exactly U = d(A,·), so the scale note is valid and vertical separation still gives the bound |U(X) − U(Y)| ≤ d(X,Y). Only the horizontal axis is warped, and that axis never carried a distance meaning.

    
Even is fine for a clean, uniform-looking cloud, but it misleads in two ways:

    
    
      
  • Equal spacing on screen is no longer equal steps of U, so the scale note says “not to scale”.
    • 
      
  • It hides how concentrated the vertices really are.
    • 
    

    
The blob stays in all layouts on purpose, since it is a true property of the graph.
